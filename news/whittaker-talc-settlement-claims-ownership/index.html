US$535 Million Talc Settlement Approved as Claimants Contest Who Owns the Claims

A bankruptcy settlement can turn on a question that precedes valuation: who has the legal right to pursue the claim being settled?

Published by The Legal Circle

October 24, 2026

New Jersey, United States; Canadian comparison

A bankruptcy settlement can turn on a question that precedes valuation: who has the legal right to pursue the claim being settled?

A New Jersey bankruptcy judge approved Whittaker Clark & Daniels’ US$535 million settlement involving Berkshire Hathaway affiliate National Indemnity on October 22.

Claimants objected that it improperly protected solvent affiliates. The judge concluded that the relevant indemnification and contribution claims belonged to the debtor. Claimant counsel plans an appeal. Reuters: settlement approval and planned appeal.

Settlement approval and claim ownership

The approval does not resolve every broader dispute about non-debtor protection. It places ownership of particular claims at the centre of this settlement.

For restructuring lawyers, that distinction is fundamental. A debtor’s claim against another company and an injured person’s independent claim against that company may involve overlapping events without being legally interchangeable.

The analysis therefore needs to identify the cause of action, the injury alleged, the person entitled to recover and the relief sought.

Separate authority from valuation

A large settlement amount cannot answer those ownership questions. Conversely, identifying an estate-owned claim does not eliminate scrutiny of whether its settlement is reasonable.

That creates two separate tasks for counsel: establish who may compromise the claim, then assess the proposed compromise.

The decision also illustrates why reporting should distinguish settlement approval from final distribution to claimants. Approval is consequential, but appeals and further bankruptcy steps can still affect implementation.

Approval is not final distribution

For Canadian practitioners, the case offers a comparative example of how claim characterization can shape restructuring leverage. Canadian law governing estate property, third-party claims and releases requires its own analysis.

The planned appeal will be important for understanding how the court’s approach survives further scrutiny.

Before asking whether a settlement pays enough, the court must identify whose legal rights are being settled.
