Air Canada Pricing Appeal Puts Consumer Remedies Before Supreme Court

A displayed price rises during booking. Even if that practice violates consumer law, what must the customer establish to obtain a price reduction?

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 16, 20262 min read
  • #Air Canada drip pricing appeal
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 16, 2026

Quebec, Canada

A displayed price rises during booking. Even if that practice violates consumer law, what must the customer establish to obtain a price reduction?

That remedial question is central to Consumers’ Union v. Air Canada, scheduled before the Supreme Court of Canada on October 16.

The underlying transaction involved a ticket price that increased by $124.46 through taxes, fees, charges and surcharges added during the booking process. The proceedings concern Quebec consumer law, available remedies and constitutional arguments about its application. No Supreme Court judgment has been released.

A prohibited practice and a monetary remedy are distinct questions

The Quebec Court of Appeal identified an error concerning the presumption of prejudice but declined to impose a price reduction because it found no basis for quantifying it. It nevertheless awarded punitive damages.

Air Canada also raised arguments concerning federal jurisdiction and the application of the provincial legislation.

The case therefore requires attention to several distinct issues: the merchant’s obligation, the proof supporting relief and the particular remedy sought.

Why quantification matters

A conclusion that conduct breaches a statute does not necessarily answer how a court should calculate monetary relief.

For consumer counsel, that makes the evidentiary theory important from the beginning. The claim must explain why the proposed remedy follows from the statutory framework and the transaction.

For businesses, the distinction should not be mistaken for reassurance that an unlawful pricing practice has no financial consequences. Different remedies can serve different purposes.

Punitive damages and a reduction of the consumer’s obligation should not be treated as interchangeable.

The implications for online pricing

Booking screens and checkout sequences create a record of what consumers were shown, when additional amounts appeared and how the transaction progressed.

Preserving that sequence can matter as much as preserving the final receipt.

Counsel reviewing a pricing process should examine the customer’s full path rather than assess each screen in isolation. The legal significance of particular taxes or charges must also be considered under the applicable rules.

The eventual judgment may clarify the relationship between proof of a prohibited practice and proof supporting a particular remedy.

A pricing rule’s practical force depends partly on what consumers must prove to obtain relief.
