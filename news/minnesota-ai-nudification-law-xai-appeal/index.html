Minnesota AI “Nudification” Law Temporarily Halted in xAI Appeal

Who should bear legal responsibility when an AI service allows someone to create a synthetic intimate image of an identifiable person?

Minnesota, United States; Canada

Who should bear legal responsibility when an AI service allows someone to create a synthetic intimate image of an identifiable person?

Minnesota’s attempt to place prevention duties on service providers has encountered an appellate setback. On October 2, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit granted xAI an injunction pending appeal, temporarily halting enforcement of the state’s “nudification” law. The injunction is interim relief, not a final decision that the law is unconstitutional.

The dispute deserves attention in Canada because it concerns where lawmakers place responsibility along the chain connecting a software provider, a user and the person depicted.

What Minnesota’s law targets

The law took effect on August 1. Minnesota’s official legislative summary describes restrictions on owners or controllers of websites, applications, software and services that give users access to nudification technology. It also prohibits advertising or promoting covered services.

The legislation provides civil remedies for people depicted, including damages and injunctive relief. It specifies potential civil penalties of up to $500,000 for each unlawful instance of access, download or use.

The policy choice is significant. It places obligations on businesses providing access to the technology, extending the regulatory focus beyond the individual who creates or distributes an image.

What happened in the courts

xAI argues that the measure infringes freedom of speech protected by the U.S. Constitution.

A lower court previously refused emergency relief. According to Reuters, U.S. District Judge Donovan Frank concluded that xAI had not established irreparable harm and had delayed seeking an order. The Eighth Circuit has now granted temporary protection while the appeal proceeds.

That procedural distinction should guide how the development is reported. The law’s enforcement has been paused at this stage; the constitutional challenge remains unresolved.

Why provider duties change the legal debate

A rule directed at the creator of an abusive image asks what that person did and whether the conduct attracts liability.

A prevention duty directed at a service provider introduces different questions. What functionality must the provider restrict? How should the law address attempts to evade safeguards? What evidence would demonstrate compliance or a failure to comply?

Those questions affect product design as well as litigation. A provider-facing rule can require a business to consider prohibited uses before a particular victim brings a complaint.

From a policy perspective, that approach seeks intervention earlier in the process. Its difficulty lies in defining the duty precisely enough that providers can understand what conduct the law requires.

The relevance for Canadian lawyers

The U.S. constitutional dispute does not determine Canadian law. Its comparative value lies in the regulatory choices it exposes.

Canadian discussions about synthetic intimate images can ask whether responsibility should attach to creation, distribution, access to particular tools, or several stages at once. Each approach has different implications for proof, prevention and remedies.

For counsel advising technology businesses, the case illustrates why the exact statutory duty matters. A rule governing access to a service creates a different compliance problem from a rule governing the publication of a completed image.

For lawyers representing affected individuals, the corresponding question is whether a legal framework offers a practical route to stop the harm and pursue an effective remedy.

The next stage of the xAI appeal will be worth watching. For now, the accurate headline is a temporary halt—and an unresolved dispute over how far provider prevention duties may go.
