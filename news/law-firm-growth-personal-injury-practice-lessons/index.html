Law Firm Growth: What Other Practices Can Learn from Personal Injury Firms

Advertising starts the conversation. Operations determine whether the practice can sustain it. Five steps to build a firm that can handle growth.

Analysis

Written by Adele Salikhova

October 6, 2026

Ontario, Canada

The lawyer on the billboard is easy to notice. The system behind the billboard is harder to see.

Personal injury advertising makes a practice visible: buses, roadside signs, television commercials and search results. That visibility attracts attention—and sometimes scepticism from lawyers whose practices grow through quieter channels.

But the more useful question is what happens after someone responds.

Who answers the call? How is the inquiry assessed? What happens when the lawyer is unavailable? Does someone follow up? Can the firm handle additional demand without making every client wait longer?

Advertising starts the conversation. Operations determine whether the practice can sustain it.

For lawyers considering how to grow a law firm, that distinction deserves more attention than the size of a marketing budget.

More inquiries can expose a weak business

Imagine a firm whose website begins generating twice as many inquiries.

The partners initially see success. Soon, however, calls interrupt legal work, prospective clients repeat their stories to several people, and messages accumulate in a shared inbox. Nobody knows which inquiries received a response.

The marketing worked. The firm was unprepared for the result.

This is a common growth trap: investing in demand before establishing how to handle it.

A practice can have excellent lawyers and an unreliable client journey. Expertise does not automatically create an effective intake process, clear responsibilities or timely communication.

The Law Society of Ontario’s practice review guidance connects effective communication with client service and encourages lawyers to provide information proactively. That supports a broader operational point: communication needs an organized place in the practice.

The practice cannot depend on one person remembering everything

In a small firm, the founder may attract clients, assess inquiries, perform the work and manage relationships.

That arrangement can function well at a certain size. As demand grows, however, the founder becomes the point through which too many decisions must pass.

An intake question waits for the partner. A routine update waits for the partner. A potential referral waits for the partner to remember a conversation from last week.

The solution is to distinguish work requiring legal judgment from work that can follow an established process.

Scheduling, collecting appropriate preliminary information, confirming receipt and explaining administrative next steps can have clear owners. Legal assessment and advice remain with the professionals responsible for them.

The aim is to give lawyers more capacity for the decisions clients retain them to make.

A measurable client journey creates better decisions

Revenue tells a firm something happened. It rarely explains the entire path.

A useful operational picture includes where inquiries originate, how quickly the firm responds, whether matters fit the practice and where prospective clients stop progressing.

Suppose paid advertising produces many calls but few suitable matters. Increasing the budget may amplify the problem.

Alternatively, suitable inquiries may arrive consistently but receive slow responses. The priority then becomes intake capacity.

Without that distinction, a firm can spend money trying to solve the wrong problem.

Five steps to build a practice that can handle growth

1. Map the inquiry-to-retainer journey

Write down what happens from first contact to an accepted engagement.

Include calls, website forms, referrals, preliminary assessment, conflict checks and engagement documentation. Identify handoffs and places where an inquiry can stall.

Keep the map simple enough for the team to use.

2. Assign responsibility at each stage

Every stage needs a named owner.

Who acknowledges a website inquiry? Who arranges the consultation? Who follows up when requested information is missing?

A shared inbox can be a useful tool, but it is not an accountable person.

3. Create clear intake criteria

Define the matters your firm handles and the information needed to assess an inquiry appropriately.

Give intake staff a process for escalating questions. Ensure administrative screening does not drift into legal advice.

Better intake should help people reach the appropriate next step.

4. Track a few useful measures

Start with inquiry source, response time, suitability and progression to an engagement.

Use consistent definitions. Review the results regularly enough to identify problems while they can still be addressed.

A small set of reliable measures is more useful than a dashboard nobody trusts.

5. Test capacity before increasing demand

Consider what would happen if next month brought substantially more suitable inquiries.

Could the team respond promptly? Could lawyers assess and undertake the work? Would existing clients receive the same attention?

Resolve the obvious constraints before expanding the campaign.

The lesson extends beyond personal injury

Different practices have different economics, professional obligations and client needs. A commercial boutique should not automatically copy another firm’s advertising model.

It can still ask the operational questions that visible marketing raises.

Can the practice turn attention into an organized response? Can it maintain service as demand increases? Can clients move through the process without depending on one overloaded lawyer?

The strongest growth strategy is one your firm can deliver after the phone rings.
