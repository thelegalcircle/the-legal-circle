Canada Opens Charity Regulation Consultation on CRA Enforcement and Revocation

Finance Canada and the CRA seek feedback on charity registration, sanctions and revocation. Submissions close December 3, 2026.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 10, 20264 min read
  • #Canada charity regulation consultation
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 10, 2026

Canada

Should failing to file an annual return cost a charity its registration, or should the Canada Revenue Agency have a more effective intermediate response?

That is one of the questions in a federal consultation launched on October 8. Finance Canada and the CRA are seeking views on registration, digital services, audits and compliance, and revocation and objections. Submissions close December 3, 2026.

For lawyers advising charities, the exercise reaches well beyond updating online services. It could influence enforcement tools, procedural protections and the information made available to the public.

The enforcement ladder is under review

The consultation paper asks whether monetary penalties or suspension should be used instead of revocation for non-filing organizations. It also raises questions about re-registration following serious non-compliance.

The paper reports that 1,219 organizations lost registration for failing to file in 2025–26, while approximately 30% reapplied during the same period.

These are consultation questions, not adopted changes.

Charities should continue to meet existing requirements. The consultation does not create a filing exemption or promise a less severe response to non-compliance.

Proportionality has practical consequences

An enforcement system needs to distinguish between different kinds of failure.

A missed filing caused by weak administration presents a different problem from deliberate misuse of charitable resources. The response must protect the system while creating a realistic route back to compliance where appropriate.

For counsel, the policy question is how legislation can make those distinctions workable.

Relevant considerations include the seriousness of the breach, the organization’s response, repeat conduct and the consequences for beneficiaries who depend on its services.

Transparency must be designed with fairness

The consultation also considers whether more information about compliance activity should be public.

Greater disclosure can support donor confidence. It can also cause reputational harm if findings are published without adequate context or before the relevant process has concluded.

A useful framework would need to address what is disclosed, when it becomes public, how disputed findings are described and how later corrections are communicated.

Those questions make procedural fairness part of the design, rather than something considered only after enforcement begins.

Counsel can contribute concrete evidence

The consultation provides an opportunity to explain where current processes create avoidable difficulty.

Strong submissions can identify a recurring problem, describe its practical consequences and propose a workable change. Examples should be anonymized appropriately and supported by evidence where possible.

Useful topics include filing barriers, unclear decision letters, objection procedures and the relationship between digital systems and volunteer-run organizations.

Modernization will require more than software

Improved online services may reduce friction. They cannot alone answer how sanctions should operate or what public disclosure should contain.

The more consequential work concerns the relationship between support, enforcement and accountability.

For charities and their advisers, the December deadline offers a chance to influence that relationship before legislative or administrative changes are settled.

A modern charity framework needs both effective enforcement and a credible path to compliance.
