Former U.S. Immigration Judge Alleges Pressure to Reverse Bond Decisions

When the government disagrees with an adjudicator, the route it takes to challenge the decision matters.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 25, 20262 min read
  • #immigration judge independence

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 25, 2026

United States; Canadian administrative-law comparison

When the government disagrees with an adjudicator, the route it takes to challenge the decision matters.

Former U.S. immigration judge Karen Donoso Stevens told The Washington Post that a supervisor directed her to reverse bond decisions without the government bringing a reconsideration motion or pursuing the usual appeal process.

The October 25 investigation reports her allegations; it is not a judicial finding of interference. The Justice Department said adjudication is impartial and governed by law and evidence. The officials contacted did not answer the specific allegations. The Washington Post: Donoso Stevens’s account and DOJ response.

The process is part of the protection

The institutional question extends beyond whether a particular bond decision was legally correct.

A formal challenge gives the parties an identifiable argument to address. It creates a record and places the proposed outcome before the decision-maker through a recognized procedure.

An alleged instruction conveyed through a supervisor raises different concerns: who initiated it, what authority supported it, whether the parties could respond and whether the adjudicator remained free to reach an independent conclusion.

Those are questions raised by the account - not findings that the reported conduct occurred.

Independence inside the executive branch

U.S. immigration courts operate within the executive branch. That structure makes the distinction between administrative supervision and control over individual decisions especially consequential.

Managers must be able to organize workloads and administer a tribunal. The difficult boundary arises when supervision allegedly reaches the outcome of a particular case.

For Canadian administrative-law practitioners, the comparative issue is familiar even where the governing institutions and legal protections differ: what arrangements preserve independent judgment while permitting legitimate institutional management?

Relevant safeguards can include clear communication rules, recorded reconsideration procedures and an effective way to raise concerns about attempted influence. Their value lies in making the decision-making process visible and reviewable.

The reported allegations warrant scrutiny without being treated as established misconduct. Neither a contested decision nor a general assurance of impartiality answers every question about how that decision was reached.

A fair adjudicative process needs more than a defensible result. It needs a defensible route to that result.
