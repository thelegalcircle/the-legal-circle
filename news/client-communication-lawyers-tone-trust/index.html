Client Communication for Lawyers: Why Your Tone Builds Trust

Five practical ways to improve tone, explain uncertainty and make your firm’s service promise credible.

By Adele SalikhovaAnalysisOctober 6, 20267 min read
  • #Client Communication
  • #Client Trust
  • #Law Firm Marketing
Legal Marketing & PR

Analysis

Written by Adele Salikhova

October 6, 2026

Ontario, Canada

A successful tax lawyer once gave me advice that sounded almost too simple to explain her client relationships.

“Watch your voice.”

She had many happy clients. When I asked what made the difference, she spoke about calmness, warmth and precision: how she greeted people, answered their questions and helped them approach a difficult problem.

That advice stayed with me because legal marketing usually concentrates on what happens before the first conversation. Firms invest in websites, search visibility, credentials and polished biographies.

Then a prospective client calls.

At that moment, the firm’s promise becomes a human interaction. The person answering can make the caller feel comfortable asking a difficult question—or make them feel that they should already know the answer.

For lawyers, tone is part of how expertise becomes usable.

The client is assessing more than your legal knowledge

A prospective client may be comparing qualifications. They may also be wondering whether they can afford the work, whether their problem is serious enough to justify calling, and whether they will be judged for the decisions that brought them there.

An experienced business owner can still feel exposed discussing a tax problem. A sophisticated executive can still struggle to explain a family dispute.

Consider two responses to a confused client:

“We already explained that in the engagement letter.”

Or:

“The engagement letter covers this, but let’s walk through what it means for your situation.”

Both direct the client to the same information. The second also gives them permission to understand it.

That matters because a client who feels embarrassed to ask questions may leave a conversation without grasping the advice. A lawyer’s explanation has limited practical value if the client cannot confidently use it.

Warmth needs substance behind it

Friendly language alone will not build a reliable client relationship. A cheerful greeting followed by unanswered emails creates its own credibility problem.

The useful combination is warmth, precision and follow-through.

Warmth makes questions welcome. Precision explains the position, including its limits. Follow-through demonstrates that the conversation meant something.

Ontario’s professional conduct rules reinforce the underlying service obligation. Rule 3.2-1 requires courteous, thorough and prompt service; its commentary addresses responding to communications, reporting developments and maintaining contact when clients reasonably expect it. Law Society of Ontario

The marketing implication follows from that professional foundation: a firm’s client experience should support the claims on its website.

If the website promises clear communication, the consultation should explain the next step. If it promises responsiveness, someone should own the callback.

Reassure clients about the process

Tone becomes dangerous when reassurance slips into a promise the lawyer cannot keep.

“Don’t worry. We’ll fix everything” may sound comforting, but it creates expectations before the facts, risks and options have been assessed.

A more useful response is:

“I understand why this is concerning. We need to review the documents before advising on the outcome. Here is what we can do next.”

That response acknowledges the concern, preserves uncertainty and gives the client something concrete.

Clients do not need manufactured confidence. They need a clear account of what the lawyer knows, what remains unresolved and what happens next.

Five ways to improve client communication

1. Review your opening sentences

Look at how your team begins calls, consultations and emails. Does the opening welcome the question or immediately defend the firm?

“Thank you for flagging this” creates room for discussion. “As previously advised” can close that room before the explanation begins.

Choose language appropriate to the situation; forced enthusiasm can feel dismissive when the matter is serious.

2. Explain before correcting

When a client misunderstands something, identify the point of confusion before repeating the conclusion.

Try: “There are two separate deadlines here. Let me explain which one applies to your next step.”

The aim is to make the distinction understandable without turning the exchange into a test the client has failed.

3. Finish with a clear next step

Close important conversations by confirming who will do what, by when, and when the client should expect an update.

“The team is reviewing it” leaves a gap. A specific commitment gives the client a workable expectation.

4. Make communication a team standard

Clients experience the whole firm: reception, intake, legal staff, lawyers and billing.

Review common situations together, including fee questions, missing documents and delayed responses. Agree on how to explain them clearly while preserving appropriate boundaries.

5. Invite useful feedback

Ask clients whether they understood the next step and felt comfortable raising questions.

Compliments are welcome, but specific feedback reveals where communication needs work. Repeated requests for clarification may signal an explanation that needs rewriting.

The tax lawyer’s advice was memorable because it brought an abstract idea—client trust—down to something observable in every conversation.

Your credentials may earn the first call. Your voice helps determine whether the client asks the question that matters.

Source: Law Society of Ontario Rules of Professional Conduct, chapter 3.
