ECHR Opinion Warns Against Automatically Excluding an Absent Lawyer’s Defence

A disciplinary regulator may require a lawyer to attend personally. But absence alone does not necessarily justify refusing to hear the lawyer’s representative.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 21, 20262 min read

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 21, 2026

Luxembourg and European Convention on Human Rights; Canadian comparison

A disciplinary regulator may require a lawyer to attend personally. But absence alone does not necessarily justify refusing to hear the lawyer’s representative.

That is the central procedural lesson from an October 20 advisory opinion of the European Court of Human Rights concerning Luxembourg’s lawyer-discipline system.

The request concerned the relationship between mandatory personal attendance, representation by counsel and the ability to obtain a fresh hearing after an adverse default decision. ECHR: advisory opinion announcement.

A meaningful opportunity to defend

The Court explained that a disciplinary body must examine whether the person unequivocally waived participation or sought to evade proceedings. Where neither is established, it must provide a genuine opportunity to present a defence, potentially through an adjournment or representation in the person’s absence. HUDOC: opinion and procedural safeguards.

The availability of a later remedy also matters. Preventing counsel from being heard, while providing no effective opportunity for a fresh adversarial examination of factual and legal issues, can upset the balance required by Article 6’s fair-hearing guarantee. HUDOC: opinion and procedural safeguards.

Attendance requirements and safeguards

The opinion does not create an unrestricted entitlement to ignore disciplinary hearings. Its reasoning requires attention to the circumstances of the absence and the safeguards available across the proceedings.

For Canadian professional regulators, it offers a useful comparative discussion rather than a binding domestic rule. The Protocol No. 16 opinion is advisory; the requesting Luxembourg court must apply the guidance to the proceedings before it.

The broader governance question is how a regulator can enforce participation requirements while preserving a meaningful defence.

What regulators should record

A robust default procedure should make the decision-maker address what notice was given, what explanation was offered, whether counsel is available and what review remains possible. Recording those findings helps explain why proceeding without the individual is justified.

The severity of the possible outcome makes that discipline particularly important. Administrative efficiency and professional accountability both depend on a process capable of producing a defensible decision.

An attendance requirement can support accountability. Treating every absence as surrender of a defence can undermine it.
