EU Council Backs Greater ESMA Supervision of Major Financial Markets

Europe’s effort to integrate its capital markets is moving toward a different allocation of supervisory authority.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 9, 20263 min read
  • #ESMA supervision
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 9, 2026

European Union; Canadian implications

Europe’s effort to integrate its capital markets is moving toward a different allocation of supervisory authority.

On October 9, the Council of the European Union agreed on key elements of its negotiating position on the Market Integration and Supervision Package. Its position would transfer supervision of the most significant cross-border trading venues and post-trading entities, including central counterparties and central securities depositories, to the European Securities and Markets Authority. Consilium

For Canadian businesses operating in Europe, the proposal could eventually change which regulator oversees important parts of their activities. It has not changed those responsibilities today.

This is a negotiating position

The Council’s agreement is a step in the legislative process. Negotiations with the European Parliament will begin once the Parliament adopts its own position. The final provisions and implementation arrangements are therefore unresolved. Consilium

Headlines suggesting that ESMA has already taken over the relevant entities would overstate the development.

Counsel should distinguish the Commission’s proposal, the Council’s position and any eventual legislation. Differences between them may be commercially significant.

Centralization would be selective

The Council’s position would limit immediate direct ESMA supervision of crypto-asset service providers to the most significant cross-border providers. It would also refine criteria for direct supervision and establish a full-time executive board. Consilium

The qualification matters. The proposal does not place every financial business or crypto provider under a single European supervisor.

National authorities would continue to have a role. The central question for an individual business would be whether its activities fall within the final criteria.

Fewer interfaces do not necessarily mean fewer obligations

From a Canadian perspective, greater centralized supervision could make some cross-border relationships more consistent. It could also concentrate scrutiny and create different reporting or engagement expectations.

Those are potential consequences, not established results.

A business currently operating through several European entities would need to assess the proposed rules against its actual structure. An integrated market does not automatically produce a simple compliance model for every participant.

The practical work begins with mapping exposure

Counsel can prepare by identifying the entities, licences and infrastructures relevant to the client’s European operations.

That exercise should include both direct exposure and dependencies. A Canadian institution may be affected through a trading venue, clearing arrangement, custodian or European affiliate even if it is not itself a proposed direct-supervision target.

The next step is to monitor the negotiated criteria and transition provisions rather than reorganize around an unfinished proposal.

Procurement plans, expansion strategies and regulatory budgets can incorporate the possibility of change without assuming its final form.

Integration changes the compliance conversation

The development reflects a broader European ambition: reducing barriers to cross-border financial activity while making supervision more consistent.

For lawyers, the important work will be translating that ambition into entity-specific advice once the rules are settled.

Europe is negotiating a new supervisory map. Businesses still need to establish where they sit on it.
