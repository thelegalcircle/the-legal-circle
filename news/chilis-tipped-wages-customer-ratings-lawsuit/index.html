Chili’s Lawsuit Connects Tipped Wages and Customer Ratings to Discrimination Claims

Customer feedback can influence earnings. A new lawsuit asks whether that connection exposes workers to unlawful discrimination and harassment.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 24, 20262 min read
  • #Chili’s tipped wage lawsuit

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 24, 2026

New York, United States; Canadian comparison

Customer feedback can influence earnings. A new lawsuit asks whether that connection exposes workers to unlawful discrimination and harassment.

Three former Chili’s employees filed a proposed class action against operator Brinker International on October 23. They allege that reliance on tips and customer ratings creates discriminatory working conditions, including through scheduling and table assignments.

The allegations are untested. Brinker had not responded to Reuters’ request for comment when the report was published. Reuters: Chili’s proposed class action, via MarketScreener.

Untested allegations about pay and ratings

The claim draws attention to how compensation and performance systems interact.

An employer may regard a customer rating as a neutral measure of service. But the relevant governance questions include what the rating measures, how it affects the worker and whether management examines potentially biased feedback before acting on it.

For employment counsel, a useful review follows the consequence of a low score. Does it reduce access to desirable shifts? Affect assignments? Trigger discipline? Is there a meaningful route to challenge the underlying complaint?

Trace the consequence of a customer score

Harassment procedures require similar attention. A reporting policy may have limited practical value if workers reasonably fear that rejecting abusive conduct will reduce their earnings or opportunities.

Those are issues to investigate; the filing does not prove that any particular system is unlawful.

The US tipped-wage framework also requires jurisdiction-specific analysis. Canadian employers should not treat the lawsuit as a ruling on Canadian wage rules.

Keep the jurisdictional limits clear

Its broader relevance lies in the use of third-party feedback in employment decisions. When a business converts customer preferences into workplace consequences, it needs to understand what information it is relying on.

The litigation will test the plaintiffs’ attempt to connect the pay structure and rating practices to legally actionable harm.

Customer feedback becomes an employment-law issue when the employer uses it to decide who earns what.
