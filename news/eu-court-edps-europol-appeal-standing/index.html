European Union

Final appellate admissibility ruling

An independent regulator can disagree with legislation affecting its supervisory work without necessarily having standing to challenge it. That is the institutional limit illustrated by the Court of Justice’s November 17 judgment in EDPS v. Parliament and Council, C‑698/23 P.

The European Data Protection Supervisor challenged transitional provisions permitting Europol, under conditions, to continue processing certain data it had already received. The Court upheld dismissal of the action as inadmissible. It did not decide whether the contested provisions or Europol’s underlying data practices were lawful on their merits.

The dispute followed an EDPS decision requiring Europol to categorise data within prescribed periods or erase it. After the legislature adopted the transitional provisions, the supervisor argued that they undermined its independence and the effectiveness of that decision.

The Court held that the EDPS is not an EU institution entitled to bring an annulment action without demonstrating standing. It found that the challenged provisions did not alter the supervisor’s tasks, powers or independence and therefore did not directly affect its legal position.

For privacy and public-law counsel, the distinction is between disagreement with a legislative intervention and satisfying the procedural conditions for having that intervention reviewed. Before developing the merits, a claimant must identify the route that permits the court to hear them.

The outcome also calls for restraint in describing the judgment. “Privacy challenge dismissed” is accurate only with the procedural explanation attached. It would be misleading to turn that result into a judicial endorsement of data retention.

The appeal ended at the courthouse door, leaving the substantive dispute unanswered in this action.

Sources: Court of Justice announcement, C-698/23 P
