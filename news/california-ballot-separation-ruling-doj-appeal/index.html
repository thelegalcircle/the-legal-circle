DOJ Appeals After Judge Rejects California Ballot-Separation Request

A federal judge has rejected the U.S. Justice Department’s emergency request to require California to separate and withhold certain mail ballots from counting.

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 29, 2026

United States; Canadian litigation comparison

A federal judge has rejected the U.S. Justice Department’s emergency request to require California to separate and withhold certain mail ballots from counting.

U.S. District Judge David O. Carter denied the temporary restraining order on October 28. The department appealed and is seeking prompt intervention from the Ninth Circuit.

The dispute concerns people federal officials identify as likely noncitizens. That description is contested and does not establish that every listed person is ineligible or has voted.

What changed since the earlier hearing?

The earlier appellate proceeding arose before Carter had ruled. On October 26, the Ninth Circuit dismissed that appeal for lack of jurisdiction because the district court had not been given adequate opportunity to decide the request.

Carter has now issued a decision, creating a materially different procedural position for the renewed appeal.

Why the judge refused emergency relief

Reporting on Carter’s decision describes concerns about disrupting an election already underway, the reliability of the federal list and the risk of disenfranchising eligible voters.

The Justice Department argues that continued ballot processing makes urgent intervention necessary. California disputes both the requested remedy and its factual basis.

The order addresses emergency relief. It does not finally resolve the entire lawsuit or determine the citizenship of everyone on the list.

Why the procedural distinction matters

Emergency litigation can produce several different decisions in rapid succession: whether an appeal is properly before the court, whether temporary relief is justified and whether a claim ultimately succeeds.

Those decisions should not be collapsed into a single verdict on the underlying allegations.

For Canadian litigators, the comparison concerns the relationship between evidence, urgency and remedy. The more disruptive the requested intervention, the more consequential the court’s assessment of what the available record actually establishes.
