Toronto Bike-Lane Advocates Plan Supreme Court Leave Application

Does the Charter constrain a government decision to remove infrastructure that people rely on for safety?

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 14, 20263 min read
  • #Toronto bike-lane Supreme Court appeal
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 14, 2026

Ontario, Canada

Does the Charter constrain a government decision to remove infrastructure that people rely on for safety?

Cycling advocates want the Supreme Court of Canada to consider that question.

Cycle Toronto and two individual applicants announced on October 13 that they would seek leave to appeal after the Ontario Court of Appeal overturned their trial-level victory concerning provincial bike-lane removal legislation. The Supreme Court has not agreed to hear the appeal.

The proposed appeal raises a constitutional issue broader than the merits of any particular transportation policy.

The argument concerns government-created risk

The applicants’ position centres on the Charter rights to life and security of the person and the consequences of removing protected cycling infrastructure.

The announcement follows competing lower-court outcomes: a trial decision favouring the cyclists and an appellate decision favouring the province. The advocates are seeking another level of review.

The constitutional issue should be framed carefully. Asking whether government action that increases danger can attract Charter scrutiny is different from asserting a general constitutional entitlement to a particular infrastructure design.

That distinction will matter to how the proposed appeal is understood.

Leave comes before the merits

An announcement that parties intend to seek leave does not establish that an application has been filed, that leave will be granted or that the underlying arguments will succeed.

The immediate procedural question is whether the Supreme Court will hear the case. A constitutional ruling would require a further stage.

That also means the announcement itself should not be described as suspending the appellate result or preventing removal work. Any request for interim protection would require separate consideration.

For readers, these procedural distinctions prevent an intended appeal from being mistaken for an accepted one.

Evidence and legal responsibility must connect

The case presents a familiar challenge in public-law litigation: connecting evidence about consequences to a legal limit on government action.

A policy can be contested on safety, cost or effectiveness grounds without every objection becoming a constitutional claim. Conversely, describing a decision as transportation policy does not answer the constitutional argument against it.

The legal analysis must identify the challenged state action, the protected interest allegedly affected and the basis for the claimed constitutional breach.

For practitioners, that places considerable importance on how the evidentiary record and legal theory fit together.

Why lawyers beyond Toronto should follow it

If the Supreme Court grants leave, its treatment of the issues could interest counsel working on government decisions affecting public safety in other settings.

That possible relevance should remain qualified. The scope of any eventual decision would depend on the questions before the Court and its reasoning.

For now, the development is an intention to seek permission to appeal—not a Supreme Court endorsement of either side.

The next question is whether the Court will hear the case. The larger question is how constitutional scrutiny applies when government removes an existing safeguard.
