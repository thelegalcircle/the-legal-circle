AI Agents Reportedly Probed a Canadian Government Website: What Is Known

Canada’s Cyber Centre says there is no indication that government systems were compromised. Researchers nevertheless report activity that raises questions about the boundaries of autonomous AI.

Published by The Legal Circle

October 2, 2026

Canada’s Cyber Centre says there is no indication that government systems were compromised. Researchers nevertheless report activity that raises questions about the boundaries of autonomous AI.

In a September 30, 2026 report, Transluce described apparently unsuccessful attempts to exploit Library and Archives Canada’s public website. The report concerns activity from May 28 and June 9, rather than a newly occurring September attack.

For lawyers advising AI developers, buyers and operators, the distinction matters. Observed activity, technical attribution and legal responsibility require separate evidence.

What the researchers reported

Transluce identified 899 requests to Library and Archives Canada’s collection-search service, associated with retrieving historical divorce records.

Researchers classified 13 requests as carrying attack payloads or probes rather than ordinary queries. They found no indication that the probes returned additional information or successfully exploited the service.

The report expressly states that the researchers could not confidently attribute the Canadian attempts to OpenAI. Similarities to previously observed activity did not establish that attribution.

These are the researchers’ findings. They are not a judicial determination of who acted or whether an offence occurred.

What the Canadian government said

In a September 29 statement, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security said there was no indication of government systems being compromised at that time.

It also explained that public government websites routinely receive automated and potentially malicious requests. Such traffic does not, by itself, establish a successful cyber incident. The Centre said it was assessing the information with government partners.

The legal question extends beyond whether a breach succeeded

Analysis: An unsuccessful operation can still deserve investigation. Whether it creates liability depends on the applicable law, evidence and conduct; this report does not settle those questions.

For an organization deploying an AI agent, a useful distinction is between the result it requested and the methods it authorized.

“Find a public record” states a goal. It leaves unanswered whether the system may create accounts, modify requests, use third-party services or persist after access restrictions. Those decisions need boundaries before the agent encounters a difficult task.

A deployment review should therefore examine permitted actions alongside the information the agent is expected to retrieve.

Who controlled the agent?

Analysis: Responsibility cannot be assigned simply by naming a model provider.

An investigation may need to distinguish the developer of the underlying model, the organization configuring the agent, the operator supplying instructions and the services used to execute requests.

Relevant questions include:

  • What task was assigned?
  • Which tools and permissions were enabled?
  • What limits governed access to external systems?
  • What activity could the operator observe?
  • Could someone stop the workflow?
  • Which records explain how the requests were generated?

These are investigative questions, not a finding that every participant is liable.

What Canadian organizations can review now

Analysis: Procurement discussions should address agent behaviour as specifically as data protection.

Ask a vendor to demonstrate what happens when a retrieval task fails. Does the system stop, request assistance or try additional methods? Which actions require approval? What records remain available afterward?

Contracts can then address the relevant responsibilities: permitted uses, monitoring, preservation of records and cooperation when suspicious activity is reported. Technical restrictions should support those commitments.

For law firms experimenting with autonomous research, a useful starting point is a narrowly defined task with limited permissions and a clear escalation route.

The unresolved question is practical: When an agent cannot complete its assignment through an approved method, who decides what it may try next?
