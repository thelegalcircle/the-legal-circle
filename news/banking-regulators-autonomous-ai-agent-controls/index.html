Banking Regulators Ask Whether Autonomous AI Agents Can Be Controlled

Giving an AI agent permission to act is one decision. Being able to reconstruct—and stop—its actions is another.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 13, 20264 min read
  • #banking AI agent controls

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 13, 2026

United States and Europe; Canadian implications

Giving an AI agent permission to act is one decision. Being able to reconstruct—and stop—its actions is another.

Reuters reports that U.S. banking regulators are questioning institutions about controls over autonomous AI agents, including limits, monitoring, approval and auditability. European supervisors are also examining resilience and recovery preparations.

The report describes supervisory scrutiny based on sources familiar with the inquiries. It does not announce a new generally applicable rule or establish an enforcement finding against a bank.

For financial-services counsel, the questions point toward an evidence problem: can the institution demonstrate how an agent’s authority is governed?

Authorization must have boundaries

A conventional software tool may support a human decision. An autonomous agent can take a sequence of actions toward an objective with less direct intervention.

That makes the scope of its authority important.

Counsel and risk teams need to understand which systems it can access, what transactions it can initiate and when it must obtain approval. A broad instruction to improve efficiency is not a complete description of permitted conduct.

Consider a hypothetical agent tasked with resolving a customer issue. It might retrieve records, communicate with the customer and propose an adjustment. Those are different activities, potentially requiring different controls.

The relevant governance question is whether its permissions reflect those differences.

An audit trail must explain the action

A record that an agent completed a task may be inadequate when an institution needs to investigate an error.

A useful record should allow reviewers to identify the instruction, relevant inputs, actions taken and approvals obtained. It should also help distinguish the agent’s activity from subsequent human decisions.

For legal teams, that information can support incident investigation, contractual analysis and responses to supervisory questions.

The organization must determine whether it can retrieve the necessary records from its own systems or depends on an external supplier to provide them.

Stopping the agent is only part of recovery

A shutdown mechanism can prevent further action. It may not reverse changes already made.

Recovery planning should therefore examine the state of affected systems, transactions and records. It should identify who decides whether operations can resume and what evidence supports that decision.

That is particularly important when several automated systems interact. Disabling one component may leave consequential activity running elsewhere.

The practical goal is to understand containment and restoration as separate tasks.

What Canadian financial-services counsel can review

The reported foreign inquiries do not automatically establish Canadian requirements. They nevertheless provide concrete questions for internal governance:

  • Authority: Who approved the agent’s role and permissions?
  • Limits: Which actions require human authorization?
  • Records: Can the institution reconstruct significant decisions and actions?
  • Containment: Who can suspend the agent, and how quickly?
  • Recovery: How are affected operations restored and checked?

Counsel should also examine whether supplier contracts provide the access and cooperation those controls require.

The central issue is not whether an institution has adopted AI. It is whether the institution can explain and govern the authority it has delegated.

An agent’s ability to act should never exceed the organization’s ability to account for its actions.
