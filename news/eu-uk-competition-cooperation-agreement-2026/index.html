EU-UK Competition Agreement: What Coordinated Enforcement Could Mean for Businesses

Separate competition authorities can investigate the same business conduct. A formal cooperation agreement gives that parallel scrutiny a more structured framework.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 19, 20262 min read
  • #EU UK competition cooperation agreement
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 19, 2026

European Union and United Kingdom; Canadian implications

Separate competition authorities can investigate the same business conduct. A formal cooperation agreement gives that parallel scrutiny a more structured framework.

The EU-UK competition-cooperation agreement was published in the EU’s Official Journal on October 19, following its signature on February 25. The publication is the new development; the agreement was negotiated and signed earlier.

Its framework covers notifications about relevant investigations, coordination of enforcement activities and information sharing. The treaty preserves independent enforcement and subjects cooperation to legal safeguards. Sharing is discretionary, with confidentiality and data-protection restrictions remaining relevant. Treaty text, including Article 14 (PDF).

Publication is not commencement

Publication must also be distinguished from commencement. Article 14 makes entry into force dependent on completion and notification of the parties’ approval procedures. It specifies commencement on the first day of the second month following the month of the last notification. Treaty text, including Article 14 (PDF).

For businesses facing scrutiny in both jurisdictions, the practical implication is preparation for proceedings that may interact more closely without becoming a single investigation.

Cooperation does not necessarily produce identical legal tests, timetables or outcomes. Counsel will still need to assess each authority’s powers and the particular proceeding.

Coordinate the factual record

However, submissions prepared in isolation can create avoidable difficulties. A factual explanation offered to one authority should be checked against the position taken elsewhere. Differences may be justified, but they should be understood and deliberate.

Confidentiality decisions also require attention. Before consenting to an exchange, a business should understand the proposed information, recipient, purpose and applicable protections. A general preference for faster coordination should not replace that assessment.

A coordinated cross-border response

For Canadian companies with UK and EU operations, the agreement reinforces the value of a coordinated internal response: a shared factual record, consistent document preservation and clear responsibility for communications across jurisdictions.

It also creates a useful distinction for client advice. Authorities may cooperate more closely while retaining independent responsibility for their decisions. Companies should prepare for both features.

The investigations remain separate. The response should account for how they may connect.
