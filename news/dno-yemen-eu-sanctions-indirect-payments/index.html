DNO Yemen Ruling Highlights Indirect Sanctions Risk in Award Payments

A payment’s named recipient may be absent from a sanctions list. That does not necessarily end the inquiry.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 16, 20262 min read
  • #DNO Yemen sanctions ruling
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 16, 2026

European Union; Canadian comparison

A payment’s named recipient may be absent from a sanctions list. That does not necessarily end the inquiry.

The Court of Justice’s October 15 decision in DNO Yemen, C‑842/24, concerns EU sanctions and payments to unlisted Yemeni public entities under an arbitration award.

The reported judgment addresses whether sanctioned parties could obtain the funds indirectly. Its approach requires evidence of a meaningful diversion risk rather than treating competing claims of control as an automatic presumption.

Recipient screening is a starting point

Checking the recipient’s name answers one question: whether that entity is itself listed.

It may leave unanswered who can access the account, direct the money or obtain the benefit of payment.

For award-enforcement counsel, the distinction can be consequential. An obligation to pay and the permissibility of a particular payment route require separate consideration.

Neither the existence of an award nor the absence of the recipient from a list supplies the entire sanctions analysis.

Risk needs an evidentiary foundation

The reported decision emphasizes practical access to funds and the evidence supporting the risk of benefit to sanctioned persons.

That matters in both directions. Counsel need to examine evidence suggesting diversion, alongside safeguards or circumstances that prevent access.

A general description of political instability may provide context, but it does not replace transaction-specific analysis.

The relevant inquiry should remain tied to the applicable prohibition and the facts of the proposed payment.

What counsel should assemble

A payment review may require information about the recipient’s governance, account arrangements, payment instructions and controls over subsequent transfers.

Where those facts are disputed, counsel should identify what is known, what remains uncertain and which assumptions materially affect the conclusion.

Documenting that reasoning also makes the assessment easier to revisit if circumstances change.

Canadian relevance

The decision interprets EU sanctions. It does not determine whether a payment complies with Canadian law.

Cross-border matters may require separate analysis under several regimes. A conclusion under one system should not silently become the conclusion under another.

The wider lesson is to examine the real payment pathway, rather than stop at the formal recipient.

Sanctions analysis must follow the money far enough to assess who can actually benefit.
