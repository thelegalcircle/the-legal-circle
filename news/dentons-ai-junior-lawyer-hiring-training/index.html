Dentons Says AI Has Not Changed Junior Hiring - But the Law-Firm Pyramid May Shift

A law firm can maintain junior hiring today while reconsidering how many junior lawyers it will need tomorrow.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 12, 20264 min read
  • #Dentons AI junior lawyer hiring
Business & Practice Development

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 12, 2026

Global; Canadian implications

A law firm can maintain junior hiring today while reconsidering how many junior lawyers it will need tomorrow.

That distinction runs through Dentons global CEO Kate Barton’s interview with Financial News. Barton said AI had not yet affected first-year hiring, while acknowledging that future recruitment might grow more slowly than the firm. She also described a strategy emphasizing sector expertise, lateral recruitment and stronger coordination of major client relationships. Her comments describe Dentons’ position, rather than establish an industry-wide hiring trend.

For Canadian legal professionals, the useful question is what happens between those two points: stable recruitment now and a potentially different staffing model later.

Headcount and opportunity are different measures

A firm does not have to announce layoffs for its employment model to change.

If its business expands while entry-level recruitment remains steady, junior lawyers can represent a smaller proportion of the workforce. That is a different outcome from cutting an existing cohort, but it can still affect the number of opportunities available to new graduates.

It also changes what firms need from each recruit.

Where technology reduces time spent on routine production, the remaining work may demand more interpretation, client communication and supervision. The management challenge is to determine whether junior lawyers receive enough experience to develop those abilities.

Hiring numbers alone cannot answer that question.

Training requires deliberate design

Some routine work is repetitive. Some is also educational.

A first draft can teach a junior lawyer how facts connect to a legal test. Reviewing documents can develop an understanding of how a transaction unfolded. Research can reveal why an apparently relevant authority does not resolve the problem.

The value lies in the reasoning the task requires, rather than its duration.

A firm introducing AI therefore needs to identify which learning experiences it wants to preserve. Otherwise, it may remove work that helped lawyers develop judgment without creating a replacement.

One possible approach is to require juniors to formulate the issue and proposed analysis before reviewing generated material. Another is to have them explain why they accepted, revised or rejected an AI suggestion.

Those exercises make supervision about reasoning, rather than simply checking the finished document.

Client coordination adds another pressure

The strategic emphasis on coordinated client relationships also has a workforce implication.

A lawyer serving an institutional client needs to understand its business, recognize when another practice group should be involved and communicate across offices. Efficient document production is only one component of that service.

For Canadian firms, this creates a useful management question: are recruitment, training and compensation supporting the kind of collaboration the firm expects to deliver?

A stated commitment to client service needs a structure that makes cooperation practical.

What law-firm leaders should measure

A more informative assessment of AI’s workforce effects would examine:

  • Recruitment relative to growth: Are entry-level opportunities keeping pace with the business?
  • Learning opportunities: Which tasks have disappeared, and what replaces their educational value?
  • Supervision quality: Can juniors explain and defend the work they submit?
  • Client exposure: Are developing lawyers gaining opportunities to understand commercial objectives and communicate advice?

These measures would reveal more than a simple declaration that AI has - or has not - reduced hiring.

Barton’s qualified position leaves the longer-term outcome open. The immediate issue is whether firms can redesign work while continuing to develop the lawyers they will eventually need.

A firm’s future depends on what its juniors learn, as well as how many it hires.
