AI Errors in Judicial Orders Lead to Reassignment in Mississippi DEI Case

Unverified AI material in a judicial order can affect far more than the court’s reputation. In a Mississippi case, the consequences now include reassignment and the loss of preliminary relief.

Mississippi, United States; Canadian comparison

Unverified AI material in a judicial order can affect far more than the court’s reputation. In a Mississippi case, the consequences now include reassignment and the loss of preliminary relief.

The US Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated an injunction against Mississippi’s DEI legislation on October 22 and directed that the case return to a different district judge. Fifth Circuit: Jackson Federation of Teachers v. Fitch (PDF).

The appellate court identified purported parties who were not in the case, unsupported factual assertions and a nonexistent precedent. Judge Henry Wingate had attributed errors in an earlier order to a law clerk’s AI-assisted drafting and the mistaken filing of a draft.

The injunction and reassignment

The Fifth Circuit also examined deficiencies in the injunction’s legal analysis. Its decision should therefore not be reduced to a conclusion that identifying an AI error automatically determines the underlying dispute. Fifth Circuit: Jackson Federation of Teachers v. Fitch (PDF).

The ruling does not finally resolve the constitutionality of Mississippi’s law. It changes the interim remedy and who will manage the case on remand.

For Canadian lawyers and judges, the decision offers a concrete comparison with current discussions about judicial AI governance.

Verification beyond citations

Verification must cover the record as well as the authorities. A real citation cannot support a finding about evidence that was never filed. Similarly, correcting obvious names or dates does not establish that the reasoning has been independently reassessed.

The episode also highlights the importance of preserving a transparent procedural record when an issued order requires correction. Parties and appellate courts need to understand what changed and why.

For institutions introducing AI, the relevant safeguards extend beyond training individual users. They include drafting controls, source checks, document approval and a clear process for responding when an error reaches the public record.

Institutional controls remain human

The responsibility remains human, even where the initial mistake originates in a tool.

Judicial reasons must be grounded in the case the parties actually brought and the evidence actually before the court.
