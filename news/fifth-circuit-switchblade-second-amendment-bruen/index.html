Fifth Circuit Applies Second Amendment Framework to Federal Switchblade Restrictions

The US constitutional framework governing weapons regulation reaches beyond firearms.

By The Legal CircleAnalysisOctober 23, 20262 min read
  • #Fifth Circuit switchblade ruling
Legal Developments

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

October 23, 2026

United States; Canadian comparison

The US constitutional framework governing weapons regulation reaches beyond firearms.

In an October 21 decision, the Fifth Circuit held that switchblades qualify as “arms” under the Second Amendment and rejected central interstate restrictions in the Federal Switchblade Act.

Applying New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen, the court concluded that the government had not established the required historical justification for the challenged restrictions. Fifth Circuit: Knife Rights v. Blanche (PDF).

Separate provisions, separate outcomes

The disposition was divided by statutory provision. The court reversed dismissal of the challenge to section 1242, affirmed dismissal of the separate facial challenge to section 1243 and remanded for further proceedings. Fifth Circuit: Knife Rights v. Blanche (PDF).

That distinction limits the headline. The decision should not be reported as eliminating every federal restriction involving switchblades.

Its broader significance lies in the analytical sequence: whether the weapon falls within the constitutional text, followed by the government’s burden to justify the regulation through the applicable historical framework.

The historical-justification framework

For litigators, the case illustrates how the definition of the protected category can shape the evidence needed to defend modern restrictions.

It also demonstrates the importance of separating claims within a constitutional challenge. A court may accept the central rights argument while rejecting another claim because its requirements have not been satisfied.

For Canadian readers, the ruling concerns US constitutional law. It does not alter Canadian restrictions on prohibited weapons or authorize importing an item into Canada.

Keep the US ruling within its scope

Its comparative interest is methodological. Courts applying a history-based test must decide which earlier regulations are sufficiently analogous and how those analogies relate to the modern measure.

Further proceedings remain, and any practical account of the ruling must identify the provision and activity at issue.

The decision extends the analysis to knives while preserving important limits in the court’s actual disposition.
